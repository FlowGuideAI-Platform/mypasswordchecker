Four ways passwords actually fall

Dictionary attack — seconds

Lists of real passwords from previous breaches run first — millions of candidates in seconds. Anything on those lists falls immediately, regardless of length. That is why the test above flags a password containing one of the ten thousand most common.

Brute force — the exponential clock

Every combination, in smart order, at the GPU rate printed under the figure above. The cost doubles with every bit of entropy — which is why the table below goes from "instant" to "longer than the universe" in twelve characters.

Rainbow tables — lookup speed

Precomputed hash-to-password lookups. Modern salting defeats them, but unsalted leaks fall at lookup speed — effectively instant for any password the table covers. Randomness keeps you out of the table; cleverness does not.

Credential stuffing — no cracking at all

Your exact password from one breach, replayed against your other accounts. Strength is irrelevant — reuse is the vulnerability. One password per site, and check yours against known breaches.

Cracking, answered plainly

How long does it take to crack a password?

Anywhere from under a second to longer than the universe has left. Eight lowercase letters fall instantly; twelve mixed characters hold a GPU rig for tens of thousands of years. The table on this page shows the cliff — every added character multiplies the work.

What is a dictionary attack?

Trying lists of real passwords from previous breaches before any random guessing. Millions of candidates run in seconds, so anything on those lists falls immediately, no matter how long it is.

My password was in a breach — does strength still matter?

Not for that password. Credential stuffing replays your exact leaked password against your other accounts, so strength is irrelevant — reuse is the vulnerability. Change it everywhere it was used and never reuse it.

Do attackers really try every combination?

Only as a last resort. Real crackers order guesses by likelihood: dictionaries first, then common patterns and substitutions, then raw brute force. Entropy still sets the budget — a truly random password forces them into the slow lane.

Does quantum computing break passwords?

Grover's algorithm halves effective strength — 80 classical bits become 40. The machines to run it at scale do not exist yet, which is why our quantum figure is labelled a future-proofing estimate, with its assumed rate printed beside it.
